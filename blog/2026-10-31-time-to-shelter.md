# Time to Shelter: How Close Does Your Bunker Need to Be?

*A missile's flight time is fixed. Everything else, from the alert to the blast door, comes out of the same few minutes. Our new tool shows how much distance that leaves.*

The question we are asked least, and should be asked most, is not how deep a shelter is. It is how long it takes to get into it.

A shelter protects the people inside it. On the day it matters, that only includes the people who reached it in time. So we have built a free tool, [Time to Shelter](/simulators/time-to-shelter), that sets the clock against the journey.

## The clock you do not control

A long-range ballistic missile takes about 30 minutes from launch to impact. One launched from a submarine close to a coast can arrive in as little as 10 to 15. Those are round figures, and real flight times depend on the missile and the distance it flies, but they set the scale. Britain's Cold War planners expected to give the public four minutes. You are working in minutes, not hours.

## Everything else comes out of those minutes

The tool splits your side of the timeline into four parts. Three of them are spent before you travel a metre.

- **The alert.** Someone has to spot the launch, decide to warn the public and get the warning to your phone. No government publishes how long that takes. The tool starts at 3 minutes, and you can change it.
- **Getting out of the door.** Two minutes with a bag by the door. Five for a family at home. Ten if people are asleep, or there are young children or older relatives to help.
- **The journey.** Walking, cycling, or driving on clear roads or in traffic. When an alert goes out, everyone else gets in their car too.
- **Getting inside and sealed.** Opening up, getting everyone in, going down and closing the blast door behind you. The tool starts at 3 minutes.

## What the numbers say

Take a 30-minute flight, a 3-minute alert, 5 minutes to get going and 3 minutes to seal up. That leaves 19 minutes to travel. In that time a shelter can be:

| How you travel | Furthest the shelter can be |
| --- | --- |
| Walking, 5 km/h | about 1.6 km |
| Cycling, 15 km/h | about 4.8 km |
| Car, clear roads, 50 km/h | about 16 km |
| Car, everyone leaving at once, 15 km/h | about 4.8 km |

A shelter 20 km away by car looks close on a map. In this scenario you would still be about 1.7 km from the door when the missile lands.

Now change one thing: a submarine-launched missile with a 12-minute flight. The same alert, preparation and sealing leave 1 minute to travel. That is about 800 metres on clear roads and less than 100 metres on foot. If people are asleep and need 10 minutes to get moving, there is no time to travel at all.

## Minutes or days

There are two kinds of warning, and they lead to different plans.

**Minutes** means a missile already in the air. Only a shelter you can reach in that time counts, so it has to be close to where your family lives or works.

**Days** means tension building in the news. Then distance hardly matters, because you travel early and stay. That is what a planned move, and [FORTES evacuation transport](/fleet), is for.

Most households need an answer to both. A shelter for the minutes has to be near. A shelter for the days can be wherever the ground and the risk are best. The [Location Risk Checker](/simulators/location-checker) helps with the second question: how far a site is from likely targets.

## If you will not make it

The tool is blunt about this. Do not set off on a journey you cannot finish. Get inside the nearest solid building, go to the basement or the middle of it, away from windows, and stay there. Fallout takes time to arrive, so the first minutes indoors matter most. US guidance puts it in six words: get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.
